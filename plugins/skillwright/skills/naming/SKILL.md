# Naming

Anchor the decision in who encounters the name, what they need to do with it, and which mistaken expectation matters. Reuse an existing brief; ask only for missing information that changes the choice. Separate what the name must convey from what a subtitle, description, or stable identifier can carry. Distinguish a brand that builds identity from a label that helps someone find or operate something; brand meaning can be learned without the name directly describing the function.

Carry the brief across rounds: retain accepted language, naming architecture and tone, rejected candidates and their reasons, and unresolved checks. Apply a new preference together with those decisions, not in their place. Treat a liked example as evidence about qualities to preserve, not permission to copy its suffix or abandon the rest of the brief. Ease of pronunciation does not imply familiar vocabulary, a change of language, or a colloquial tone. Do not invent hard limits such as one syllable or a letter count to stand in for that preference. Describe specific likely reading or hearing difficulties as predictions, not as facts about an untested audience; shortlist tradeoffs must still respect the requested ease of use.

For brand generation, explore associations before ranking words. Connect a distinctive product behaviour to concrete images or practices outside its category; test whether metaphor, double meaning, cultural allusion or gentle incongruity yields a natural connection. Use whichever fits the brief, not a mandatory quota of devices. Show the image and its specific connection for promising directions. Different synonyms for a function are not different creative directions, and an elaborate story cannot manufacture a connection the word does not support. Keep brand generation separate from shortlisting: screen promising candidates before recommending them; if none survive, return to another direction rather than revive a rejected name. A rejected name may illustrate a quality only when clearly separated from the adoptable shortlist.

Deliver a naming decision proportional to the request:

- A brief stating the primary use, audience, language/channel, and binding constraints.
- Candidates representing different semantic directions when generating options, not spelling variants of one idea. For each shortlisted name, give its reading, intended association, likely misunderstanding, and relevant expansion risk.
- A recommendation with tradeoffs and rejected alternatives, judged against the primary use after screening hard constraints. Respect a user-supplied shortlist; do not manufacture extra candidates just to fill a format.
- Outstanding checks and a concrete way to resolve them. For a rename, include the cost of changing recognition, references, and stable identifiers; for a series, test an additional member against the naming axis.

Distinguish attested etymology from a proposed creative association. Cite a source for historical origin claims; a later interpretation does not establish the original naming motive.

Judge sound, recognizability and misleading expectations in normal context before explaining a brand story; do not require a new brand to reveal its full meaning unaided. Test the story separately for a natural, memorable connection. Keep liking, recall, correct understanding, oral transmission, and retrieval separate; choose checks for the actual task rather than an invented universal score. A story cannot compensate for failing a binding constraint.

State which checks were actually performed and what remains untested. An agent's prediction or simulated audience is not user research. If no real users were tested, say so in the decision. Commercial availability needs scoped, current checks; an unperformed search is not clearance. Recommend provisionally when evidence is incomplete, without claiming legal availability or measured superiority.
